A

three questions, after reading closely · tap one

from Predicting regional neurodegeneration from the healthy brain functional connectome  ·  Zhou et al., Neuron, 2012  ·  and Additive effects of cerebrovascular disease functional connectome phenotype and plasma p‑tau181…  ·  Chong et al., Alzheimer’s & Dementia 20(12), 2024, where you are corresponding author  ·  and projects 2.1, 2.33 and 3.2.

The 2012 result turned functional connectivity into a predictor of regional vulnerability, with a node’s connectivity to an epicentre forecasting its eventual decline. The 2024 paper then computes whole-brain functional connectivity in a cohort recruited through memory clinics, where cerebrovascular burden is close to universal.

BOLD is a haemodynamic signal, and the haemodynamic response function is not constant across people or across tissue. Two regions can show a weaker correlation because their coupling changed, or because their response functions drifted apart. Those are different findings, and a correlation computed under a canonical HRF cannot tell them apart.

This is precisely what my final-year thesis estimated: subject-specific HRFs from resting-state fMRI in the ADNI cohort, treating the vascular component of the signal as something to be measured rather than regressed away.

the one piece of my own work that bears directly on yours.

The analysis finds one phenotype associated with high burden across lacunes, cortical infarcts, microbleeds and white matter change together. That is a clean result and it buys enormous statistical power over testing four markers separately.

But those four have partly distinct vascular mechanisms. A single latent variable loading on all of them may be reporting overall severity rather than a mechanism, in which case the connectome phenotype is a disease-stage marker wearing network clothing.

The test is not a different method, it is a different question asked of the same decomposition: do the marker-specific residuals carry any connectivity signal of their own? Splitting costs power. Not splitting costs interpretation. Knowing which one you are paying is worth an analysis.

the decomposition already exists. this reuses it.

If the vascular and amyloid axes really are independent at baseline, they are two separate pieces of predictive information rather than one quantity measured twice, which is why the additive model works as well as it does.

Whether they stay independent across the two-year and four-to-five-year imaging follow-ups is a different question, and it is one the cohort can already answer. If the axes converge over time, a score that simply adds them will drift out of calibration exactly when it matters most.

The appeal of this one is that it needs no new acquisition and no new participants. The scans to test it have been collected.

already-acquired follow-up, not a new study.

B

why this group

Four years of inferring internal states from the outside, through whatever channel happened to be available. A dog’s bark. A child’s gaze. A radio wave off a moving body. A BOLD time course.

Every one of those taught the same lesson: the transfer function between the state and the signal is where the errors live. Your group is the first place I have seen where that is treated as the research question rather than as preprocessing.

C

the same coupling, two different response functions

The underlying neural coupling below is identical in both settings. The only thing that changes is the haemodynamic response function of the second region, delayed and broadened the way vascular disease does. Watch the correlation coefficient. Nothing about the network changed, and the connectivity estimate moved anyway. y(t) = h(t) ∗ s(t) + ε

matched response functions. the estimate recovers the true coupling.

Simulation. One shared neural source, convolved with two response functions, plus independent noise. Drawn to make the mechanism visible, not to quantify its size in any cohort.

D

what the reading buys

If a measurable part of a connectivity difference is haemodynamic, then HRF variation is a confound with a direction: it will look like reduced connectivity, specifically in the people with the most vascular disease, which is exactly the contrast of interest.

That is a correctable problem rather than a fatal one. Subject-specific HRF estimation, or deconvolution before computing connectivity, puts the vascular term in the model instead of leaving it in the residual.

It also turns a nuisance into a variable. If the HRF itself tracks vascular burden, it is a candidate marker, measured from resting-state data that has already been acquired, with no extra sequence and no extra scanner time.

I am not claiming this explains your result. I am claiming it is testable in your existing data, and that I have already written the code that tests it.
